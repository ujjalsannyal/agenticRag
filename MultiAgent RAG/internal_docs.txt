Climate change disrupts ecosystems across the planet, forcing wildlife to adapt to rapidly shifting temperatures, disappearing habitats, and altered food chains. While polar creatures face the most immediate and dramatic losses due to melting sea ice, terrestrial, marine, and freshwater animals globally are also experiencing severe population declines and behavioral shifts.

Impacts on Polar Creatures

The Arctic and Antarctic regions are warming at a rate roughly two to four times faster than the global average. Because polar species have evolved specialized traits for extreme cold, even minor temperature increases threaten their survival.
• Polar Bears: These apex predators rely on sea ice as a platform to hunt ringed and bearded seals. As spring ice melts earlier and autumn ice forms later, polar bears face longer fasting periods, leading to lower body weight, reduced cub survival rates, and increased human-wildlife conflict as bears venture into coastal towns for food.
• Emperor Penguins: Emperor penguins require stable, land-fast sea ice to breed and raise their chicks. Premature ice breakups in Antarctica have caused catastrophic breeding failures, where thousands of chicks drown or freeze to death before developing waterproof feathers.
• Walruses: Pacific walruses use floating sea ice to rest between dives for clams and other seafloor invertebrates. When summer sea ice retreats over deep ocean waters where they cannot reach the bottom, tens of thousands of walruses are forced onto crowded coastal beaches, resulting in deadly stampedes when startled.
• Arctic Foxes: Warming temperatures allow larger, more aggressive red foxes to expand their range northward into the tundra. Red foxes outcompete Arctic foxes for food and den sites, and they occasionally prey on them directly.
• Narwhals and Belugas: Reduced sea ice opens Arctic waters to commercial shipping, oil exploration, and underwater noise pollution. This acoustic disruption interferes with the echolocation and communication these whales depend on to navigate and find prey.

Impacts on Marine Animals

Oceans absorb roughly 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases and about a quarter of human-produced carbon dioxide, triggering warming, acidification, and deoxygenation.
• Coral Reefs: Prolonged marine heatwaves cause corals to expel the symbiotic algae living in their tissues, a process known as coral bleaching. Without these algae, corals turn white and starve, destroying habitats that support roughly 25% of all marine species.
• Sea Turtles: For most sea turtle species, the temperature of the sand where eggs are buried determines the sex of the hatchlings. Warmer beaches produce overwhelmingly female populations, threatening long-term genetic diversity and reproduction, while rising sea levels erode nesting beaches.
• Krill and Baleen Whales: Antarctic krill feed on algae growing beneath sea ice. Declining sea ice shrinks krill populations, which directly starves the blue whales, humpback whales, and seals that rely on krill as their primary food source.
• Shellfish and Pteropods: Excess carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering ocean pH. Ocean acidification reduces the availability of carbonate ions, making it difficult for oysters, clams, lobsters, and tiny sea butterflies (pteropods) to build and maintain their shells.
• Cold-Water Fish: Species like Atlantic cod and salmon require cool, oxygen-rich water to thrive. Warming waters force these fish to migrate toward the poles or into deeper waters, disrupting commercial fisheries and starving predators like puffins that cannot dive deep enough to catch them.

Impacts on Terrestrial Animals

On land, rising temperatures, prolonged droughts, and extreme weather events alter plant growth cycles and force animals to shift their geographic ranges.
• Amphibians: Frogs, salamanders, and toads have permeable skin that makes them highly sensitive to temperature and moisture changes. Shifting rainfall patterns dry out breeding ponds before tadpoles can metamorphose, while warmer conditions accelerate the spread of lethal fungal diseases like chytridiomycosis.
• Pikas: The American pika is a small mountain-dwelling mammal adapted to cold alpine environments. Because pikas can die from overheating if exposed to temperatures above 78°F (25.5°C) for just a few hours, they are forced to retreat higher up mountain slopes until they run out of habitable terrain.
• African Elephants: Increased frequency and severity of droughts dry up seasonal watering holes and reduce vegetation. Elephants require massive amounts of water and food daily, leading to higher calf mortality and increased migration into agricultural areas.
• Caribou and Reindeer: Warming Arctic winters occasionally bring freezing rain instead of snow. This forms a hard layer of ice over the tundra, preventing caribou from digging through to reach the lichens they eat to survive the winter.
• Migratory Birds: Many bird species time their spring migrations and breeding seasons to coincide with peak insect availability. As warmer springs cause insects to hatch earlier, migratory birds often arrive after the peak food supply has passed, leaving less food for their chicks.

Broader Ecological Consequences

• Trophic Cascades: When a keystone species like krill, coral, or caribou declines, the effects ripple throughout the entire food web, causing secondary extinctions and ecosystem collapse.
• Phenological Mismatches: Climate change alters the timing of seasonal biological events—such as flowering, hibernation, and migration—at different rates for different species, breaking apart interdependent relationships between predators and prey or plants and pollinators.
• Increased Disease Transmission: Milder winters and expanded tropical zones allow disease-carrying vectors like mosquitoes and ticks to survive year-round and spread into new territories, exposing wildlife to pathogens for which they have no immunity.
